# Michigan fan website audit

**Reviewed:** August 19, 2026  
**Perspective:** A Michigan fan evaluating whether the site is exciting, useful, trustworthy, and easy to understand.  
**Scope:** Homepage, global navigation, team, players, schedule, recruiting, analytics, articles, predictions, game pages, history, methodology, polls, and supporting routes.

## Executive read

The site has the ingredients of a Michigan destination I would bookmark: a strong point of view, real roster depth, unusually honest labeling, useful historical context, and an editorial layer that tries to explain what the numbers mean. It feels most distinctive when it answers a football question instead of displaying a database.

The biggest issue is product coherence. The site currently feels like three experiences living together:

1. a Michigan editorial publication called **Michigan Football Focus**;
2. an analytics product called **SOAR**;
3. an older utility-style data application that still appears on several secondary routes.

As a fan, I can use all three, but I do not yet understand their relationship. The homepage promises a confident, premium season guide. Some clicks fulfill that promise; others lead to pending modules, duplicate schedule presentations, thin explanations, or routes that redirect elsewhere.

My short verdict: the site has a compelling Michigan brain, but it needs one identity, fewer unfinished doors, and a clearer hierarchy between editorial analysis, current-season reference material, and advanced analytics.

## What I like

### 1. The site speaks in football questions

The strongest copy is specific and consequential:

- “The run game gave Michigan enough. Drives still ended too quietly.”
- “Who matters most to Michigan’s ceiling?”
- “Four things Michigan must make travel.”
- “Can a proven culture unlock Michigan’s young core?”

Those are the questions Michigan fans actually debate. They make the data feel like evidence in an argument instead of a pile of statistics.

### 2. The Michigan–Utah comparison is the clearest analytical story

The main analytics page has a strong thesis: Michigan and Utah were both run-led, but Utah was better on third down, generated more explosive runs, and finished scoring opportunities. The contrast between Michigan’s No. 18 success-rate rank and No. 78 points-per-opportunity rank is memorable. It gives me a concrete reason to believe the new staff could improve the offense without changing Michigan’s basic identity.

The boundary statement—Utah is staff context, not a Michigan projection—is excellent. It increases trust.

### 3. Player pages handle uncertainty responsibly

The distinction among actual production, recruiting benchmarks, projected roles, and missing evidence is one of the site’s best features. Freshmen are not assigned fake college production grades. Players without measurable box-score usage are not treated as failures. Transfers retain their previous-team context.

That is more credible than most fan sites and should remain a defining product principle.

### 4. The history section feels like a real destination

“The Vault” has the best sense of place. It offers an obvious starting point, standout seasons, chronological browsing, team records, achievements, schedules, stats, postseason context, and rosters. It encourages exploration instead of presenting one dead-end table.

As a Michigan fan, this is where I am most likely to lose 20 minutes clicking through seasons.

### 5. The homepage knows the current team’s central characters

Leading with Bryce Underwood, Jordan Marshall, Savion Hiter, and the new staff is the correct editorial choice. The homepage is not trying to give equal visual weight to 100 roster entries. It identifies the players and relationships that will define the season.

### 6. The schedule presents uncertainty honestly

Market information only appears where sourced information exists, and the copy explains that the win chance is market-calibrated rather than a proprietary prediction or betting recommendation. Missing lines remain missing.

### 7. Articles connect claims back to product evidence

The “Data Behind the Story” treatment is a meaningful differentiator. An article can send me directly to the relevant player, schedule, analytics comparison, or roster surface. That makes the publication feel internally connected.

### 8. Navigation is understandable at the top level

Home, Team, Players, Schedule, Recruiting, Analytics, and History are sensible primary destinations. Mobile reduces that list and keeps “More” available. The information architecture is much easier to understand than a menu organized around internal model names.

## What I do not like

### 1. The brand relationship is unclear

The repository mandate identifies the product as **SOAR Analytics**, but the navigation, metadata, footer, and much of the public copy identify it as **Michigan Football Focus**. The homepage then says “The SOAR Season Preview.”

As a fan, I cannot tell whether:

- SOAR is the analytics engine powering Michigan Football Focus;
- Michigan Football Focus is a section inside SOAR;
- the product is being renamed;
- or two concepts were combined without a final decision.

This is the highest-level problem because it affects every page. Pick one public product architecture and explain it once. A sensible model would be “Michigan Football Focus, powered by SOAR Analytics,” but that must be an intentional product decision rather than an inferred compromise.

### 2. “The New Age Era” is awkward language

“New age” and “new era” communicate nearly the same thing. Combined, the phrase sounds accidental rather than iconic. “A New Age,” “The Whittingham Era,” or “New Command” would be cleaner.

The homepage also repeats “new era” in several nearby places. Repetition reduces the impact of the lead concept.

### 3. Too many routes promise features that are not there yet

The main analytics page is substantive, but its deeper offense, defense, situational, and charts routes are mostly waiting states. The portal page says arrivals and departures are coming soon even though the product already has transfer-status data. Game pages say the matchup breakdown is coming later. Coaching analytics largely says tracking starts after kickoff.

An unfinished module is acceptable. A cluster of unfinished destinations makes the site feel earlier-stage than the homepage suggests. Hide or consolidate thin routes until they provide a meaningful fan answer.

### 4. There are duplicate ways to reach similar information

`/schedule` and `/games` both present the 2026 schedule. `/predictions` and game pages overlap with schedule market information. `/analytics`, `/metrics`, `/rankings`, and `/compare` collapse into the same destination through redirects.

Redirects preserve old links, but these routes should not appear to be separate product concepts in documentation or navigation. Choose canonical surfaces and make each one own a distinct question.

### 5. The methodology page is too thin for the site’s claims

The site uses production grades, position percentiles, success rate, points per opportunity, market-calibrated win chance, projected importance ranks, historical CFP résumé chance, and recruiting display grades. The methodology page offers only two short paragraphs.

That is not enough for a product whose credibility is a core strength. Fans do not need equations first, but they do need plain-language definitions, directionality, denominators, source dates, and links to deeper technical notes.

### 6. The homepage has two openings competing for attention

The New Age feature is the intended lead, followed immediately by another large “Michigan Football” hero with next-game information. Both act like a primary hero. The result may feel like the homepage starts twice.

The second section contains useful information, especially the next matchup, but it should behave like a compact season-status rail beneath the feature—not another full declaration of the product.

### 7. Some article inventory risks feeling generated rather than edited

The researched features have clear arguments. The automatic “The [player] question” stories are thinner and structurally repetitive. If these appear beside major reported features with equal weight, they dilute the Notebook’s editorial authority.

Treat brief player notes as a different content type, or require a stronger original thesis before calling each one an article.

### 8. The site sometimes explains its caveats more clearly than its value

The evidence boundaries are excellent, but several pages foreground “not official,” “not a forecast,” “not college production,” “not betting advice,” and “coming soon.” Those statements are necessary, yet a fan should first understand what the page *can* tell them.

Lead with the useful answer, then place the boundary close to the number it qualifies.

## What may be confusing

### 1. What does a letter grade mean?

The site uses grades for recruiting and production, but the visual similarity can imply that they measure the same thing. A five-star freshman with an “S” recruiting display and a veteran with an “S” production grade are not equivalent.

Recommended fix: use visibly different labels and treatments, such as **Prospect Tier** versus **Production Grade**, everywhere—not only in explanatory copy.

### 2. What does “position percentile” compare against?

The player board says “position percentile,” but a fan may not know whether the cohort is Big Ten players, all FBS players, returning players, players above a usage threshold, or players from one season.

Put the cohort in a tooltip or short inline label.

### 3. Is the Player Importance Board an official depth chart?

The page includes a disclaimer, but a numbered ranking with roles and player imagery feels authoritative. Fans may share a screenshot without the explanatory paragraph.

Add “SOAR PRESEASON IMPORTANCE” directly inside the repeated row or ranking header so the qualifier travels with the visual.

### 4. Why is Utah the comparison team?

Readers who enter on `/analytics` without reading the feature may not immediately know that the new Michigan staff is Utah-connected. The page says “staff context,” but the personnel bridge could be stated more explicitly near the top.

### 5. What is “points per opportunity”?

The article explains that it concerns possessions crossing the opponent’s 40-yard line, but the analytics comparison itself does not define the denominator close to the number. “Cashed in chances” is friendly language; the formal definition should still be one click or one sentence away.

### 6. Are market win chances predictions from this site?

The schedule eventually explains the source, but the homepage next-game card shows “WIN CHANCE” without “market” in the label. A fan may reasonably assume it is a SOAR model output.

Use “MARKET WIN CHANCE” everywhere the number appears.

### 7. Why do some pages look editorial and others look like utilities?

The homepage, analytics overview, articles, team page, player profiles, and history vault have authored visual identities. National recruiting tables, depth chart, game pages, pending analytics pages, methodology, polls, and “More” are much more utilitarian.

The difference can feel like moving between a finished publication and an internal tool.

### 8. Why are Predictions not in the main navigation?

Predictions exist and include market-derived CFP odds, but they are not prominent in the current navigation. If predictions are intentionally secondary, fold them into Schedule. If they are a core product promise, expose them consistently and distinguish market outlook from model forecasts.

### 9. “Top 20” versus exact rank

The analytics homepage says the offense stayed on schedule at “TOP 20,” while the underlying success-rate rank is No. 18. The rounded language is defensible, but the page uses exact ranks elsewhere. Consistency would make the evidence easier to compare.

### 10. What is available now versus after kickoff?

The product mixes 2025 actuals, 2026 preseason roster facts, sourced market benchmarks, projections, and future in-season modules. The labels are individually sound, but there is no single orientation explaining what the site currently knows.

A compact “2026 data status” note could clarify: roster and schedule are preseason; comparative performance is 2025 actual; in-season Michigan analytics begin after Week 1.

## Route-by-route notes

### Homepage

**Like:** Strong current-team focus, excellent key-player selection, useful next-game card, direct paths into the main product areas.  
**Dislike:** Two consecutive hero-scale sections; repeated “new era” language; unclear SOAR/Michigan Football Focus relationship.  
**Confusing:** Whether the market win chance is a SOAR prediction; why the lead headline is “New Age Era.”

### Team

**Like:** The playoff case is concise and supported by data. Schedule pressure and the importance board answer real preseason questions.  
**Dislike:** The importance list may become extremely long and visually repetitive.  
**Confusing:** Exact methodology behind importance ranking and how much comes from projected depth versus production.

### Players and player profiles

**Like:** Responsible evidence labels, useful past-season context, links to position rooms and related stories.  
**Dislike:** The main Players page shows only 12 production cards before sending the reader elsewhere; it feels more like a teaser than a player hub.  
**Confusing:** Grade families, percentile cohorts, and why some important linemen have no production grade.

### Schedule and games

**Like:** Clean week-by-week orientation and responsible market labeling on the schedule page.  
**Dislike:** Duplicate `/games` schedule and thin individual game pages.  
**Confusing:** “Win chance” label on the homepage and what will eventually distinguish a game page from the schedule.

### Recruiting and portal

**Like:** Recruiting is correctly framed as pedigree rather than college proof. National browsing adds real depth.  
**Dislike:** The transfer portal page is empty despite known incoming-transfer data.  
**Confusing:** Recruiting display grades resemble production grades.

### Analytics

**Like:** Best explanatory page on the site; the Michigan–Utah comparison has a clear football thesis.  
**Dislike:** Most child routes are placeholders, making the analytics ecosystem feel shallower than the overview suggests.  
**Confusing:** Bar scales, points-per-opportunity definition, and the immediate reason Utah is the chosen comparison.

### Articles

**Like:** Strong researched features, helpful filters, evidence links, and source lists.  
**Dislike:** Automatic player stories may overwhelm stronger editorial work.  
**Confusing:** Whether the Notebook is reporting, analysis, automated player notes, or all three.

### History

**Like:** Strongest complete section and best browsing loop.  
**Dislike:** The archive begins in 2010, which is practical for available data but can feel narrow for Michigan.  
**Confusing:** “Sixteen seasons” may imply the full historical vault unless “modern archive” stays prominent.

### Methodology

**Like:** Clear commitment to missing-data honesty and source-of-truth separation.  
**Dislike:** Far too brief for the number of custom metrics and grades used throughout the site.  
**Confusing:** Nearly every specific method a curious fan would want to verify.

### Polls and More

**Like:** Poll privacy is stated plainly and “More” keeps mobile navigation manageable.  
**Dislike:** A single local-only poll feels too small to justify a destination. “More” is a link directory rather than a real discovery surface.  
**Confusing:** “Community results will appear” can sound like voting is being collected centrally when the current pick stays only on the device.

## Prioritized recommendations

### Priority 1: Decide the product identity

Define the public relationship between SOAR Analytics and Michigan Football Focus. Apply it consistently to navigation, metadata, footer, article labels, and methodology.

### Priority 2: Remove or consolidate unfinished doors

Do not prominently route fans to offense, defense, situational, charts, portal, coaching analytics, or game breakdowns until each offers at least one meaningful piece of current evidence. Consolidate duplicate schedule/game/prediction surfaces.

### Priority 3: Make methodology fan-usable

Add a glossary organized around questions:

- How do production grades work?
- What is the comparison cohort?
- What is points per opportunity?
- What counts as an explosive play?
- What is actual, projected, preseason, or benchmark?
- Which numbers come from markets versus SOAR models?

### Priority 4: Simplify the homepage hierarchy

Keep the New Age feature as the clear lead. Convert the second hero into a shorter season-status band containing the next opponent, date, location, and market context. Let the player core follow immediately.

### Priority 5: Differentiate evidence types visually

Production grades, prospect tiers, market estimates, projections, and actual team metrics should never rely only on small text labels. Give each evidence family a stable visual treatment.

### Priority 6: Finish the transfer story

The roster already knows which players transferred and their previous teams. Use that evidence to build a useful portal arrivals page before presenting an empty state.

### Priority 7: Protect editorial quality

Separate long-form features, analysis notes, and automated player briefs. Lead the Notebook with edited arguments and prevent repetitive player templates from dominating the library.

### Priority 8: Add a real fan exploration loop

At the bottom of major pages, recommend the next logical question rather than only a generic destination. Examples:

- From Underwood’s profile: “How did Michigan’s passing offense perform in 2025?”
- From the Utah comparison: “Which transfers already know Whittingham’s system?”
- From Oklahoma on the schedule: “What must travel against a playoff-level opponent?”
- From a historical season: “Which current team shares this identity?”

## What I would keep untouched

- The rule against invented data.
- The separation of actual, projected, preseason, and benchmark evidence.
- The Michigan–Utah analytical thesis.
- The player-profile handling of missing usage.
- The history-vault browsing model.
- The connection between articles and underlying product evidence.
- The fan-first question framing.

## Review limitation

This audit is based on the implemented route structure, content, data presentation, responsive rules, successful production build, and runtime route checks. The in-app visual browser was unavailable, so judgments about exact spacing, cropping, contrast, scroll length, and breakpoint composition should be confirmed with screenshots at approximately 1440×900, 1024×768, and 390×844.

## Implementation status

The August 19 implementation pass addressed the audit's actionable priorities:

- unified the public shell under SOAR Analytics with Michigan Football Focus as the Michigan edition;
- simplified the homepage into one lead feature plus a shorter season-status section;
- renamed the lead “A New Age” and clarified market-derived win chance labels;
- replaced the empty transfer page with verified incoming-transfer data;
- consolidated unfinished analytics and duplicate schedule routes into useful canonical pages;
- expanded methodology into evidence types, metric definitions, cohort context, data status, and trust boundaries;
- distinguished prospect tiers, production grades, projections, actuals, and market benchmarks in high-risk sharing surfaces;
- labeled long-form features separately from lightweight player briefs;
- removed the thin polls destination from promoted navigation;
- replaced the empty game-week breakdown with contextual next steps;
- added more useful exploration paths from player and game pages.

Screenshot-based responsive verification remains outstanding because visual browser control was unavailable during the implementation pass.
